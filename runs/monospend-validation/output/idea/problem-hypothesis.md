# Problem Hypothesis

## Problem

People who want to understand their spending often abandon budget apps because entry is too structured: forms, dropdowns, categories, account setup, bank sync, subscriptions, and dashboards. Some users already think in notes, spreadsheets, or plain text, so a conventional app feels slower than simply typing "coffee 5.50" or "rent 1800" into a note.

MonoSpend's hypothesis is that budgeting can feel more natural if the primary interface is a notepad: type income, expenses, variables, and math; the app parses and calculates in real time. The problem is not "people need another expense tracker." The problem is "manual tracking is useful, but the interface is too much work for people who prefer text."

## Target customer

The first reachable customer should be narrow: privacy-conscious note takers who already track money in Notes, Obsidian, a spreadsheet, or a plain-text accounting tool but want less syntax and more instant feedback.

Do not start with the broad "anyone who wants to take control of finances" segment. That market is too broad and crowded. Also do not start with bank-sync power users; MonoSpend's offline/no-account promise is a poor fit for people who want automatic import.

## Frequency

For the right user, the problem appears daily or multiple times per week whenever they record small expenses or check remaining free cash. For the wrong user, manual tracking is a one-week experiment that gets abandoned.

Frequency is an inference. It needs customer evidence from people who currently record purchases manually and from people who tried manual tracking but quit.

## Current behavior

Current alternatives include:

- Apple Notes, Google Keep, Obsidian, or a paper notebook.
- Spreadsheets with simple formulas.
- Privacy-first offline expense trackers.
- Natural-language expense trackers.
- Plain-text accounting tools such as hledger or Beancount.
- Full personal finance apps with bank connections and dashboards.

The strongest manual alternative is still "type it in a note and do rough math later." MonoSpend must prove that live parsing, variables, categories, and budget bars are enough improvement to change behavior.

## Assumptions

- The notepad metaphor is more important than the generic privacy/offline promise.
- Users will manually log expenses if entry feels like typing notes.
- Live variables such as `savings = 30% of salary` create a meaningful "aha" moment.
- A one-time $4.99 Pro purchase is credible for this audience.
- The user trusts an offline app enough to type financial data into it.
- The first segment wants lightweight budgeting, not double-entry accounting.

## Evidence type

Facts: MonoSpend's current landing page promises a notepad-style budget interface, real-time parsing, offline use, no ads, no tracking, export, and a one-time Pro purchase.

Inference: the privacy-first expense tracker market is crowded based on many adjacent apps with similar privacy/offline claims.

Assumption: the notepad/live-math interaction is differentiated enough to overcome crowded-category risk.

Needs verification: waitlist conversion, willingness to pay, daily retention, and whether users prefer text entry over quick form entry.
